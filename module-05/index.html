THE HAKA
THAT GOT AWAY

A funny New Zealand travel story
STORY FOCUSDon't be afraid to look silly when you try to understand another culture. Laughter brings people together.
LEVEL: SIMPLE READ · LISTEN · THINK · CREATE PHRASAL VERBS WITH GET PAST TENSES
📖 THE STORY

Last summer, three friends — Mike, Dave, and I — finally got to New Zealand. We had saved for two years. We wanted adventure, and we got more than we bargained for. Our second day, we visited a Maori village. It was amazing. The carvings, the stories, the food cooked in the ground. But the best part was the haka. The warriors stomped, their eyes went wide, their tongues stuck out. It was powerful. We were completely got by it. After the show, a Maori elder smiled at us. "You three looked like you wanted to try," he said. "Come on, we'll teach you a simple one." Mike, who always thinks he's a natural, was first. He puffed out his chest, tried to stomp, but his foot got tangled in a tree root. He didn't fall, but he did a weird hop that made everyone laugh. He got over his embarrassment quickly and laughed too. Then it was Dave's turn. Dave is very serious. He wanted to get it perfect. He practiced the tongue movement so hard that he accidentally bit his tongue. Not hard, but enough to make him squeak instead of roar. He got through the performance with tears in his eyes (from laughing at himself). Finally, it was my turn. I was so focused on not messing up that I forgot to breathe. I did the moves, I roared, and then… I sneezed. A huge, violent sneeze right in the middle of the haka. It was so unexpected that the Maori elders burst out laughing. One of them patted my back and said, "That's the most original haka we've ever seen!" We spent the rest of the evening with them, eating and laughing. We didn't master the haka, but we got along with everyone wonderfully. And we learned something: it's not about being perfect. It's about being brave enough to try, and to laugh when it all goes wrong. Later, Mike said, "I can't believe we got away with that. We must have looked like total idiots." But we didn't feel like idiots. We felt like friends.

❤️ FOR THE HEART AND MIND

A. Comprehension Check

  1. Where did the three friends go?
  2. What impressed them most at the Maori village?
  3. Who invited them to try the haka?
  4. What happened to Mike when he tried to stomp?
  5. What happened to Dave when he tried to do the tongue movement?
  6. What happened to the narrator during his performance?
  7. How did the Maori elders react?
  8. What did the friends learn from this experience?

B. Think & Analyse

  1. The elder said, "That's the most original haka we've ever seen!" Was he being sarcastic or kind? Why do you think so?
  2. The narrator says they didn't feel like idiots; they felt like friends. What made them feel that way?
  3. Why is it sometimes better to laugh at yourself than to be perfect?

C. Discuss & Relate

  1. Have you ever had an embarrassing moment while traveling or trying something new? Share it.
  2. Why do you think sharing a laugh with strangers can make you feel closer to them?
  3. The friends "got away with" looking like idiots. What does this phrase mean here? Is it positive or negative?
🧠 FOR LANGUAGE SKILLS

A. Vocabulary Focus — Phrasal verbs with GET

Match: got to · got over · got through · got along with · got away with
  1. got to (New Zealand) — a) managed to do something without punishment
  2. got over (his embarrassment) — b) arrived at
  3. got through (the performance) — c) had a good relationship
  4. got along with (everyone) — d) recovered from
  5. got away with (looking like idiots) — e) successfully completed something difficult

2. Fill in the blanks

  1. We finally ______ to the airport after a long delay.
  2. She was upset at first, but she ______ it quickly.
  3. I don't know how he ______ cheating on the test. The teacher saw everything!
  4. It was a hard time, but we ______ it together.
  5. Do you ______ your new classmates?

B. Grammar in Context — Past Tenses

  1. Past Simple vs Past Continuous: Find examples in the text — Past Simple (finished actions): ______, ______, ______; Past Continuous (background actions): ______, ______. Explain why each tense is used.
  2. Past Perfect: Find: "We had saved for two years." Why is Past Perfect used here? Complete: After they ______ (watch) the haka, they decided to try it themselves.
  3. Put the verbs in brackets in the correct past tense: (a) While Mike ______ (try) to stomp, he ______ (get) tangled in a root. (b) Dave ______ (bite) his tongue because he ______ (practice) too hard. (c) We ______ (not / expect) to laugh so much, but it ______ (be) the best evening.

C. Brain Workout

  1. Retell the story: In pairs, retell the story from the perspective of the Maori elder. What did you think of these three funny tourists?
  2. Act it out: In groups of three, act out the haka practice scene. Use the phrasal verbs from the lesson.
  3. Write a sequel: What do you think happened the next day? Write 3–4 sentences using at least two phrasal verbs with get.
🌍 BEYOND THE TEXT
  1. A postcard: Write a short, funny postcard from the narrator to his parents back home. Describe the haka disaster.
  2. Social media post: Imagine Mike posts a photo of the group with the Maori elders. Write his caption. Use #HakaFail #BestTripEver.
  3. AI image (optional): Create an image of the funniest moment — the narrator sneezing during the haka.
  4. Your own story: Write a short paragraph about a time you tried to impress someone from another culture and it went wrong (or right).

THE NOODLE
CHALLENGE

A spicy food challenge in Chengdu
STORY FOCUSSometimes a little boasting, a lot of spice, and a glass of milk make the best travel memory.
LEVEL: MEDIUMREAD · LISTEN · THINK · CREATEPHRASAL VERBS WITH GETPAST TENSES
📖 THE STORY

LEAD-IN

  1. Have you ever boasted that you could do something, and then regretted it?
  2. What’s the spiciest food you’ve ever tried? What happened?
  3. The phrasal verb "to get into" can mean to become involved in something troublesome. What do you think "to get into trouble" means?

THE NOODLE CHALLENGE

After New Zealand, we got the travel bug badly. The next year, Mike, Dave, and I decided to go to China. We wanted real adventure, not just tourist stuff. We got that — and a lot more.
После Новой Зеландии мы сильно «заболели путешествиями». На следующий год Майк, Дейв и я решили поехать в Китай. Мы хотели настоящих приключений, а не просто туристических развлечений. Мы их получили — и даже больше.

Our first week was amazing. We visited the Great Wall, got lost in the hutongs of Beijing, and got used to the chaos of Chinese streets. But the real test came on day eight.
Первая неделя была потрясающей. Мы посетили Великую Китайскую стену, заблудились в пекинских хутунах и привыкли к хаосу китайских улиц. Но настоящее испытание ждало нас на восьмой день.

We were at a night market in Chengdu. The smells were incredible — sizzling meat, strange spices, things we couldn’t identify. Dave, who had got over his seriousness from New Zealand, pointed at a stall.
Мы были на ночном рынке в Чэнду. Запахи были невероятными — шипящее мясо, странные специи, вещи, которые мы не могли определить. Дейв, который уже избавился от своей серьёзности после Новой Зеландии, указал на один прилавок.

"Look! They’re selling scorpions on sticks!"
«Смотрите! Они продают скорпионов на палочках!»

Mike, who never learns, puffed out his chest. "I’ll eat anything. I once ate a ghost pepper. This is nothing."
Майк, который никогда не учится на своих ошибках, выпятил грудь. «Я съем что угодно. Однажды я съел перец-призрак. Это вообще ерунда».

I should have stopped him. But I didn’t.
Я должен был его остановить. Но не остановил.

We walked past stalls selling silk worms, chicken feet, and something that looked like a century egg (it was). Then we saw it: a noodle stall with a sign that said "MALA XIANG GUO — SPICY LEVEL 5 — SIGN A WAIVER."
Мы прошли мимо прилавков, где продавали шелкопрядов, куриные лапки и что-то похожее на столетнее яйцо (это и было столетнее яйцо). И тут мы увидели её: лапшичную с табличкой «MALA XIANG GUO — ОСТРОТА УРОВНЯ 5 — ПОДПИШИТЕ РАСПИСКУ».

Mike’s eyes lit up. "That’s for me."
Глаза Майка загорелись. «Это для меня».

The owner, a tiny woman with a big smile, warned him. "Level 5 is very spicy. Foreigners usually take Level 1. You sure?"
Хозяйка, маленькая женщина с широкой улыбкой, предупредила его: «Уровень 5 очень острый. Иностранцы обычно берут первый уровень. Ты уверен?»

Mike was sure. He got himself into trouble before I could say a word.
Майк был уверен. Он влип в историю ещё до того, как я успел что-либо сказать.

The bowl arrived. It was red. Not tomato red — volcanic red. Steam rose from it, and my eyes started watering just from the smell. Mike took his chopsticks, smiled at us, and shoved a huge bite into his mouth.
Принесли миску. Она была красной. Не томатно-красной — вулканически красной. От неё поднимался пар, и мои глаза начали слезиться уже от одного запаха. Майк взял палочки, улыбнулся нам и запихнул в рот огромный кусок.

For one second, nothing happened.
Секунду ничего не происходило.

Then his face turned the same colour as the soup. His eyes went wide. He grabbed his throat. He tried to speak, but only a squeak came out. Tears streamed down his face. He got through the first five seconds, but then he panicked. He grabbed a bottle of water and drank it all. It didn’t help. Spice doesn’t dissolve in water.
А потом его лицо стало такого же цвета, как суп. Глаза широко раскрылись. Он схватился за горло. Он попытался заговорить, но издал только писк. Слёзы текли по его лицу. Первые пять секунд он выдержал, но потом запаниковал. Он схватил бутылку воды и выпил её всю. Это не помогло. Острота не растворяется в воде.

The owner laughed and handed him a glass of milk. "Milk helps," she said.
Хозяйка рассмеялась и протянула ему стакан молока. «Молоко помогает», — сказала она.

Mike drank it so fast that some got on his shirt. He sat there, breathing heavily, his face still red, his eyes still wet. Dave and I were crying — from laughter.
Майк выпил его так быстро, что немного молока попало ему на рубашку. Он сидел, тяжело дыша, с всё ещё красным лицом и мокрыми глазами. Мы с Дейвом плакали — от смеха.

"Are you okay?" Dave managed to ask between giggles.
«Ты в порядке?» — спросил Дейв, с трудом перестав хихикать.

Mike nodded weakly. "I got more than I bargained for," he whispered.
Майк слабо кивнул. «Я получил больше, чем рассчитывал», — прошептал он.

We spent the next hour drinking milk and eating plain rice, while the owner and her family laughed with us. By the end, Mike had got over the burn, and we had got along with everyone so well that they gave us free dumplings.
Следующий час мы пили молоко и ели простой рис, а хозяйка и её семья смеялись вместе с нами. К концу Майк оправился от жжения, а мы так хорошо поладили со всеми, что они угостили нас бесплатными пельменями.

Later, as we walked back to the hotel, Mike said:
Позже, когда мы шли обратно в отель, Майк сказал:

"I can’t believe I got away with that. I thought I’d never be able to eat anything again."
«Не могу поверить, что мне это сошло с рук. Я думал, что больше никогда не смогу ничего есть».

"You’re just being dramatic," Dave said. "You’re fine. Well, almost."
«Ты просто драматизируешь», — сказал Дейв. «Ты в порядке. Ну, почти».

We laughed all the way to the hotel. And for the rest of the trip, whenever someone said "spicy," Mike would roll his eyes and ask for milk. When we stopped by the noodle stall to say goodbye, the owner waved and shouted, "Hey, spicy boy! Next time — level six!"
Мы смеялись всю дорогу до отеля. А до конца поездки, когда кто-нибудь произносил слово «острый», Майк закатывал глаза и просил молока. Когда мы остановились у лапшичной попрощаться, хозяйка помахала рукой и крикнула: «Эй, острый парень! В следующий раз — шестой уровень!»

Mike shook his head and smiled.
Майк покачал головой и улыбнулся.

"No way. But thanks for the milk."
«Ни за что. Но спасибо за молоко».

STORY NOTES

Genre: Humorous first-person travel story

Level: Medium

Value focus: Boasting can get you into trouble, but laughter and friendship can turn mistakes into warm memories.

Language focus: Phrasal verbs with get; Past Simple, Past Continuous, Past Perfect.

Read the English text first. Use the Russian version as support, not as a substitute for reading.

DOWN UNDER AND UPSIDE DOWN

After China, we thought we had got used to surprises. But Australia? Australia was a whole new level of strange.

We landed in Sydney, and everything felt normal at first. Opera House. Harbour Bridge. Coffee. But then we started noticing things. The water in the toilet flushed backwards. (Okay, that's a myth, but we convinced ourselves we saw it.) The seasons were backwards — Christmas in the summer! The birds laughed like mad scientists. And the animals… oh, the animals.

We rented a camper van and drove along the coast. Mike was obsessed with seeing a kangaroo. “I want to get a selfie with one,” he said. “How hard can it be?”

Dave, who had got wise after China, said nothing. He just raised an eyebrow.

Our first night, we camped near a beach. In the middle of the night, we heard a loud thump on the roof. Then another. Then what sounded like a party. Mike grabbed a torch and went outside. He came back with his face pale.

“There’s a kangaroo on the roof,” he whispered.

“A kangaroo?” I said. “On the roof?”

“It’s jumping. It’s having fun. I think it’s laughing at us.”

We didn’t sleep much that night. In the morning, we found huge scratches on the van. Mike loved it. “We got visited by a kangaroo! This is amazing!”

The next day, we drove into the bush. We saw koalas sleeping in trees like furry potatoes. We saw wombats that looked like they had got lost on their way to a rock concert. But the strangest thing was yet to come.

We stopped at a gas station in the middle of nowhere. An old man with a leathery face and a hat full of fishing lures saw us staring at a sign that said “Beware of Drop Bears.”

“What’s a drop bear?” Dave asked.

The old man grinned. “Nasty creatures. They drop from trees and attack tourists. You gotta wear forks in your hair to scare ’em off.”

Mike’s eyes went wide. “Forks?”

“Yep. They hate the reflection.”

We didn’t believe him, of course. But that night, as we sat by the campfire, every sound made us jump. Every falling leaf became a drop bear. We got ourselves into a state of pure paranoia.

Finally, Dave said, “I think that man was joking.”

We looked at each other and burst out laughing. We had got completely fooled. There’s no such thing as a drop bear. It’s an Aussie joke for tourists.

The next morning, as the sun rose over the eucalyptus trees, I sat outside the van and thought about this strange, upside-down country. Everything was different. The animals were weird. The people laughed at us. Even the sky seemed bluer.

But somehow, it felt right. It felt like the world was showing us that there’s more than one way to do things. More than one way to live.

I thought about our trips — New Zealand, China, now Australia. We had got lost, got into trouble, got laughed at, and got over it all. And we had got closer because of it.

As we drove away, Mike said, “I still can’t get my head around a country where the squirrels have pouches.”

“Kangaroos aren’t squirrels,” Dave said.

“I know. But they kind of are. If squirrels were seven feet tall and boxed for fun.”

We laughed. And I realized: you don’t travel to understand the world. You travel to understand that you don’t understand anything at all. And that’s okay.
