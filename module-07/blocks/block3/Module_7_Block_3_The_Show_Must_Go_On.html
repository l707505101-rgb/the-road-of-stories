📖 THE TEXT
We asked three people about performances that changed them. Their answers were very different — but they all shared one thing: the memory of a moment that couldn't be repeated.
THE SHOW MUST GO ON
VOICE 1 — Leo, 58, street musician
"I've been playing saxophone on street corners for thirty years. People think I do it because I have no other choice. But I've been offered gigs in clubs. I've turned them all down.
Why? Because on the street, everything is real. You can't hide. If you're bad, people walk away. If you're good, they stop. Sometimes they cry. Sometimes they dance. Once, an old woman listened to me play 'Summertime' and told me it was the song her husband proposed to her with. He'd been gone ten years. She held my hand and thanked me.
Those moments can't be planned. They can't be recorded. They just happen. And then they're gone. But they're never forgotten."
Leo was asked if he ever wanted to be famous.
"Fame? No. Fame is a cage. I've seen famous musicians — they're surrounded by people but alone. Me? I'm alone on the street, but connected to everyone who stops. I wouldn't turn my life in for anything else."
VOICE 2 — Marta, 34, festival organizer
"Ten years ago, I started a festival in a tiny village. No big names. No sponsors. Just local musicians, poets, anyone who wanted to share something. The first year, about fifty people turned up. It rained. The sound system was turned off by the wind. Everything that could go wrong, went wrong.
But at the end, we were all soaking wet, sitting in a circle, singing. No stage. No lights. Just voices.
Now the festival is famous. Thousands of people turn up every year. Big bands want to play. Sponsors offer money. I've turned most of them down. If it becomes too professional, it'll lose its soul.
Every year, I worry: will the magic turn up again? And every year, it does. Because magic isn't made by equipment. It's made by people who are brave enough to share."
VOICE 3 — Sam, 22, student
"I saw something last week that I still can't explain.
I was walking home late at night. The streets were empty. Then I heard music. Not from a club or a bar — from an alley. I turned the corner and saw a woman playing violin. No case for money. No audience. Just her, playing in the dark.
I stood and listened. She played for about ten minutes. Then she stopped, looked at me, smiled, and walked away. She didn't say a word.
I've been to hundreds of concerts. Big stages, famous bands, incredible productions. But that moment — that random, unexpected moment — was the most honest performance I've ever seen. It wasn't turned on for anyone. It just was.
I still don't know who she was. But I think that's the point. Some performances aren't meant to be understood. They're meant to be felt."
THE FRIENDS' VERDICT
We told Mike, Dave, and the Narrator about these stories.
Mike: "So what's the moral? That we should become street musicians?"
Dave: "No. The moral is that the best performances can't be planned. They just turn up when you least expect them."
Narrator: "Like our movie?"
Mike: "Exactly. It was terrible. But it was real. And people felt something."
Dave: "They felt laughter. That counts."
Narrator: "Maybe that's what all performances should do — make you feel something. Even if it's just laughing at your friends."
Mike grinned. "Then our movie was a masterpiece."
And for once, we all agreed.

♥ FOR THE HEART AND MIND
FOR THE HEART AND MIND
A. Comprehension Check:
How long has Leo been playing saxophone on the streets?
Why does Leo prefer the street to clubs?
What happened when he played "Summertime" for an old woman?
Why did Marta start her festival?
What went wrong at the first festival?
Why has Marta turned down most sponsors?
What did Sam see in the alley late at night?
How was that experience different from the concerts he'd been to?
What do the friends agree on at the end?
B. Think & Analyse:
Leo says: "Fame is a cage." What does he mean? Do you agree?
Marta says: "Magic isn't made by equipment. It's made by people who are brave enough to share." What kind of bravery does performing require?
Sam's experience was "random" and "unexpected." Why are unplanned moments sometimes more powerful than planned ones?
The friends conclude that their terrible movie was a "masterpiece" because it made people feel something. Is that enough to make something art?
All three voices talk about connection — between performer and listener. Why is that connection so important?
C. Discuss & Relate:
Have you ever had a moment like Sam's — an unexpected performance that moved you?
If you could perform for anyone, anywhere, where would it be and what would you do?
Is there a difference between art that is "perfect" and art that is "honest"? Which do you prefer?
