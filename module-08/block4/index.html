📖 THE STORY
Level: Medium / Difficult   ·   Genre: Documentary narrative / Travel reflection
Value focus: True wealth is not gold. It is clean air, safety, a healthy childhood and the possibility of living without risk to life.
LEAD-IN
  1. What is the most extreme place you have ever heard of?
  2. If someone offered you a job that paid well but was very dangerous, would you take it? Why?
  3. What does the phrase “to count your blessings” mean?

Part 1: The Highest City

In the Peruvian Andes, more than 5,000 metres above sea level, lies a place called La Rinconada. It is the highest permanent settlement in the world. But “settlement” is a generous word. It is a mining town, built on the edge of a glacier, where the air is so thin that every breath feels like a struggle.

There are no paved roads. There is no running water or sewage system. There is no police force to speak of. And yet, nearly 50,000 people live here — because beneath the frozen ground lies gold.

Part 2: A Deadly Dream

For centuries, this gold has attracted fortune seekers from all over Peru. They come with a dream: to find a nugget, become rich, and escape. But the dream is a trap.

The gold is hidden in the mountains. To reach it, miners work in terrible conditions — no safety equipment, no fresh air, just sweat and explosives. They are not paid a salary. Instead, they work for two weeks without pay. On the 15th day, they can take as much rock as they can carry. If they are lucky, it contains gold. If not, they have worked for nothing.

Most of them find nothing.

The real poison, however, is the mercury. Miners use mercury to extract gold from the rock. The liquid metal binds to the gold. Then they burn it off, breathing in the toxic fumes. The mercury enters their lungs, their blood, their brains. It causes tremors, memory loss, and madness. It poisons the water and the soil. And it never goes away.

Part 3: No Childhood, No Future

What makes La Rinconada even more heartbreaking is the presence of children everywhere.

Young boys and girls work alongside adults. School is a luxury that few can afford. Childhood is not a time for play and learning — it is a time for survival.

At 1:06, the camera shows where the ore is processed. Families live in makeshift shelters, right next to the areas where rock is crushed and mercury is burned. The air is thick with dust and poison. The floors are covered with toxic mud. Children play in the same spaces where adults handle mercury with their bare hands.

Petya and Iris walk through these streets, their faces pale. The lack of oxygen at 5,100 metres makes every step hard. But the heaviness they feel is not just from the altitude. It is from seeing what humans will do to survive — and what they will do to their own children.

Part 4: What the City Has (and Hasn't)

What is there in the city? A small square. A few shops selling basic supplies. A church. But no hospital that can treat mercury poisoning. No school that can give these children a future. No playgrounds, no libraries, no parks.

The people are not evil. They are desperate. They know the gold is killing them, but they see no other way. As one miner says in the video, “We come for the gold. But the gold never lets us leave.”

Part 5: The Lesson

After leaving La Rinconada, Petya and Iris are silent for a long time. The contrast with their own lives — and with the lives of their viewers — is too sharp to ignore.

Back in their camper van, Iris finally speaks. Her voice is quiet, almost a whisper:

“We complain so much about what we don't have. A bigger house. A newer phone. More money. But we have clean air to breathe. We have safe food to eat. We have a future. These people have nothing — and they are not complaining. They are just trying to survive.”

Petya nods. He says, “This is not the only place like this in the world. We have seen others. But this one... this one stays with you.”

They do not tell their viewers to feel guilty. They do not ask for donations. They simply share what they saw, hoping that it will make people count their blessings.

Because sometimes, the most valuable thing you can own is not gold. It is the ability to take a deep, clean breath — and to know that you are one of the lucky ones.

♥ FOR THE HEART AND MIND

A. Comprehension Check

1. Where is La Rinconada and why is it famous?
2. Why is it difficult to breathe in this city?
3. How do miners get paid? Why is this system unfair?
4. How does mercury poisoning affect people's health?
5. What is shown at timestamp 1:03 and 1:06?
6. What does the city have, and what is it missing?
7. What lesson did Petya and Iris learn after visiting La Rinconada?

B. Think & Analyse

1. The text says, “The dream is a trap.” Why is the dream of finding gold a trap for the people of La Rinconada?
2. Why do people stay in such a dangerous place? What forces them to stay?
3. Petya and Iris do not ask for donations or tell viewers to feel guilty. Why do you think they choose to just “share what they saw”?
4. The miner says, “We come for the gold. But the gold never lets us leave.” What does this mean?

C. Discuss & Relate

1. What are three “ordinary” things you are grateful for after reading this text?
2. Do you think it is ethical to buy gold, knowing it might come from places like La Rinconada? Why?
3. Can one person make a difference in situations like this? If yes, how?

EXERCISE 2. The Change Within

Instruction: The song describes a change — from complaining to gratitude. Answer the questions about this change.

  1. Before visiting La Rinconada, the singer said: “I complain my coffee is not right.” What kind of small things do you sometimes complain about? Write down 2–3 examples.

EXERCISE 3. Your Own “Blessings”

Instruction: The song is called “Count Your Blessings.” Now it's your turn to count yours.

Part A. Make a list. Write down 5 blessings in your life — things you are grateful for. They can be big or small. Try to think of things you usually take for granted.

Part B. Share one. Choose one blessing from your list and explain in 2–3 sentences why it matters to you.
