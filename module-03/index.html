THE TEST

A realistic story about courage, persistence and learning in a different way.
LEVEL: EASYREAD · LISTEN · THINK · CREATE SCHOOL & EDUCATIONCOURAGE · PERSISTENCE
📖 THE STORY
Genre: Realistic first-person narrative
Value focus: A teacher who sees potential in a student, not a mistake. The courage to be “not good enough.”

LEAD-IN

  1. What subject is the most difficult for you? How do you feel before a test in that subject?
  2. Is it possible to be “bad at maths” but still smart? Why?
  3. Look at the title: “The Test”. What kind of test could it be — a school test or something else?
I used to believe I was stupid. Not in every subject. I could write essays. I could draw. But maths? Numbers danced on the page and laughed at me. I was the slowest in class. Everyone else finished the test in twenty minutes. I sat there, staring at the clock, my paper still white. My teacher was Mr. Harrison. He was strict. His glasses were thick. His voice was loud. When I got a D on a test, he sighed. Not angrily. Just… tired. Like he had given up on me. I gave up on myself too. Then came the final exam. I studied for three weeks. Every night. My mother made flashcards. My father tried to help, but he was worse at maths than me. I still failed. When I saw the grade — 34% — I felt nothing. I was used to it. After class, Mr. Harrison said, “Stay.” I waited by his desk. He didn't speak for a long time. He just looked at my test paper. Then he said something I never expected. “You know,” he said quietly, “you didn't get the right answers. But your working was not careless. You tried different methods. You thought. That is harder to teach than memorising formulas.” I didn't understand. “But I failed.” He took off his glasses and rubbed his eyes. “When I was your age, I failed everything. Not because I was lazy. Because I was afraid. Afraid of being wrong. Afraid of looking stupid. So I stopped trying.” He looked at me. Really looked. “You kept trying. Even when you were sure you would fail. Do you understand how rare that is?” I didn't know what to say. “Grades measure performance,” he said. “They don't measure courage. They don't measure persistence. They don't measure how many times you fall and get up again.” He handed me my paper. “This is not an F,” he said. “This is twenty-seven weeks of not giving up. That's an A in my book.” I walked home that day feeling lighter. I still couldn't solve quadratic equations. But maybe I wasn't stupid. Maybe I was just learning in a different way. Maybe that was enough.

❤️ FOR THE HEART AND MIND

A. Comprehension Check

  1. What subject was difficult for the narrator?
  2. How did Mr. Harrison usually react to his low grades?
  3. How long did the narrator study for the final exam?
  4. What grade did he get?
  5. What did Mr. Harrison ask him to do after class?
  6. What did Mr. Harrison say about the narrator's “working” (his process)?
  7. What did Mr. Harrison confess about his own school years?
  8. What did Mr. Harrison say grades cannot measure?

B. Think & Analyse

  1. The narrator says: “I gave up on myself too.” Why did he give up? What made him believe he was stupid?
  2. Mr. Harrison says: “When I was your age, I failed everything. Not because I was lazy. Because I was afraid.” Why is this confession important? How does it change the narrator's view of his teacher?
  3. At the end, Mr. Harrison says: “This is twenty-seven weeks of not giving up. That's an A in my book.” What does “in my book” mean here? Whose “book” matters more — the school's or Mr. Harrison's?
  4. The narrator still can't solve quadratic equations. But he feels “lighter.” What has changed?

C. Discuss & Relate

  1. Have you ever had a teacher who believed in you when you didn't believe in yourself? What did they say or do?
  2. Is it possible to measure courage or persistence with a grade? Should schools try?
  3. Mr. Harrison was “strict” and “loud” — but he was also kind. Can strictness and kindness exist together? Give examples.
  4. In your culture, is there enough respect for students who are “slow but persistent”? Or is speed valued more?

C. Brain Workout

  1. Reverse perspective: Imagine you are Mr. Harrison, 30 years later. You see your former student at a supermarket. He is a successful artist. Write TWO sentences of what you would say to him.
  2. Memory Chain: continue the cooperative recall chain from the story.
  3. Metaphor making: “Numbers danced on the page and laughed at me.” Create your own metaphor for a difficult subject. Begin with “For me, history is...”, “For me, grammar is...”, or “For me, PE is...”
  4. Emotional timeline: mark the narrator’s emotions before the final exam, after seeing the grade, when Mr. Harrison said “Stay”, during his confession, and on the way home. Use one word for each emotion.
🌱 BEYOND THE TEXT
  1. Imagine you are the narrator, 10 years later. Write a short letter to Mr. Harrison. What do you want to thank him for? What are you doing now? (80–100 words)
  2. Write a short paragraph about a time you failed at something — but learned something more important than success. It can be sports, art, a relationship, anything.
  3. Interview a teacher (optional, with permission): “What is more satisfying — a student who gets straight As, or a student who never gives up?” Report their answer in English.
  4. Create an image that captures the moment Mr. Harrison looks at the narrator and says: “You kept trying. Do you understand how rare that is?”
  5. This story celebrates persistence over speed. Make a list of 3–5 professions or skills where “slow learning” might actually be an advantage. Explain why.

🎤 AFTER THE SONG — CLASS PROJECT

“I Am More Than a Grade”

Type: Creative writing + video (or collage)
Duration: 1 week
Integration: English + self-esteem + psychological well-being

Your Mission: Create a short video or a digital collage (1 minute) using the song “More Than This Grade” as the soundtrack. Find or take photos that represent who you are BEYOND your grades.

What can you include?

  • Your hobbies (drawing, sports, music, dancing)
  • Your talents (cooking, helping others, making people laugh)
  • Your qualities (kindness, patience, courage, creativity)
  • A moment you were proud of that had nothing to do with school

Step by step

  1. Create a short video or a digital collage (1 minute) using the song “More Than This Grade” as the soundtrack. Find or take photos that represent who you are BEYOND your grades.
  2. Include, for example: your hobbies; your talents; your qualities; a moment you were proud of that had nothing to do with school.
  3. Step 1: Find 6–10 photos that show different parts of who you are.
  4. Step 2: Arrange them in any order you like.
  5. Step 3: Add the song as the soundtrack.
  6. Step 4: At the end, add one sentence in English completing: “I am more than a grade because...”

Examples:

  • “I am more than a grade because I am the one who helps my little brother with his homework.”
  • “I am more than a grade because I never give up, even when things are hard.”
  • “I am more than a grade because I make people laugh when they are sad.”

Teacher note

  • Help students separate their self-worth from school grades.
  • Create a safe atmosphere — some students may be vulnerable around this topic.
  • Students do not need to show their grades in the video.

Alternative formats

  • Paper collage with a class presentation under the song.
  • A letter to your future self: “Dear Me, remember that you are more than a grade...”

Class Wall of “I Am More Than”

Each student writes one sentence completing: “I am more than a grade because...” The class can read the sentences aloud or silently.

A TALE OF TWO SCHOOLS

Two teenagers. Two education systems. One important question: What kind of life do you want to build?
LEVEL: MEDIUM READ · LISTEN · THINK · CREATE COMPARATIVES & SUPERLATIVES SECOND CONDITIONAL SCHOOL SYSTEMS
📖 THE TEXT
Genre: Comparative portrait / Parallel stories
Value focus: There is no perfect system. Every system has strengths and weaknesses. What matters is not only where you study, but how you relate to learning and to yourself.

LEAD-IN

  1. Imagine a school where you study 12 hours a day, six days a week. What would be good about it? What would be difficult?
  2. Imagine a school with no exams, no grades, and a 5-hour day. Would you like to study there? Why?
  3. What do you think is the most important purpose of school?
PART 1: JI-HOON — SEOUL, SOUTH KOREA
Ji-hoon wakes up at 6 a.m. He is in school by 7:30. His first class is Korean literature. His last class ends at 4:30 p.m. But he does not go home. He goes to a hagwon — a private academy — for two more hours of maths. Then another hour of English. He gets home at 9 p.m. He eats, showers, and studies until midnight. Sometimes later. «My parents say this is normal,» Ji-hoon says. «Everyone does it. If you want a good university, you must study harder than everyone else.» South Korea has one of the highest education rankings in the world. Korean students are among the best in maths and science. But they are also among the most tired. Ji-hoon has never missed a day of school in four years. His exam scores are excellent. He will probably get into a top university. «I don't hate school,» Ji-hoon says. «I just don't remember the last time I wasn't tired.» When the interviewer asks him what he does for fun, he pauses for a long time. «I used to play the guitar,» he says quietly. «In middle school. But I stopped. There was no time.» He doesn't say it sadly. He says it like a fact. Like saying «the sky is blue» or «water is wet».

Audio: Part 1 — Ji-hoon

PART 2: ELINA — HELSINKI, FINLAND
Elina wakes up at 8:30. Her school starts at 9:15. She walks there with her friends. No uniform. No heavy backpack. Her first class is art. Then maths — but not from a textbook. They measure the shadows of trees in the schoolyard. There are no standardized tests in Finland. No school rankings. No private tutoring. Students rarely get homework. The school day is short — about five hours. «We have a saying,» Elina says. «Let everyone blossom in their own time.» Finland has one of the highest education rankings in the world. It also has one of the happiest students. «Do I want to go to university?» Elina thinks. «Maybe. Or maybe art school. Or maybe I'll travel first. I have time.» She plays guitar in a park with her friends every Friday. She is not very good yet. But she is learning.

Audio: Part 2 — Elina, Helsinki

PART 3: THE QUESTION

Two countries. Two teenagers. Both are 16. Ji-hoon has discipline, knowledge, and a clear path. He has sacrificed his hobbies, his sleep, his weekends. But he has opportunity. Elina has freedom, creativity, and mental health. She has time to discover who she is. But sometimes she worries: Am I learning enough? Will I be able to compete? Who is better prepared for life?

Audio: Part 3 — The Question

PART 4: THE TRUTH

There is no easy answer. Ji-hoon's system gives him focus and determination. But it takes his childhood — not suddenly, not cruelly, just… day by day. One missed guitar lesson. One Sunday of studying. Another Sunday. Elina's system gives her space to grow. But space can feel empty sometimes. She envies Ji-hoon his certainty. He envies her her free time. They don't know each other, but they are both asking the same question: Am I doing this right? Maybe the question itself is wrong. Maybe it's not: Which system is better? Maybe it's: What kind of life do you want to build?

Audio: Part 4 — The Truth

A. Comprehension Check

  1. What time does Ji-hoon wake up? When does he come home?
  2. What is a hagwon?
  3. What is the leading cause of death among Korean teenagers?
  4. What time does Elina's school start? How does she get there?
  5. What do Elina and her classmates do in maths class?

  7. What is the Finnish saying mentioned in the text?
  8. What does Ji-hoon NOT have in his life? (Give two examples from the text.)
  9. What does Elina sometimes worry about?

B. Think & Analyse

  1. The text says: «South Korea has one of the highest education rankings… and one of the highest student stress levels.» Why do you think these two things exist together?
  2. Elina's school has no uniforms, no rankings, short days. Does this mean it is «easier»? Is «easy» the same as «good»?
  3. Ji-hoon's best friend is the student next to him — but he doesn't know his name. What does this detail tell you about his life?
  4. The text asks: «Who is better prepared for life?» What is your answer? Why?
  5. The final paragraph says: «Maybe the question is wrong.» What is the «right» question, according to the text?

C. Discuss & Relate

  1. Which system is closer to your own school experience — Korea or Finland? What would you like to change about your school?
  2. Ji-hoon has discipline and knowledge. Elina has freedom and creativity. If you had to choose, which is more important for a good life?
  3. The Finnish saying is: «Let everyone blossom in their own time.» Is this possible in a competitive world? Should schools protect students from competition — or prepare them for it?
  4. Do you think it is possible to have both: high academic standards AND low student stress? Can you think of any country or school that does this well?
  5. At 16, Ji-hoon knows exactly what he needs to do to succeed. Elina is unsure about her future. Which one is more «successful» at 16? Does it matter?

WHAT IS EDUCATION FOR?

A philosophical collage: pedagogy, human rights, ancient wisdom and a folk story.

LEVEL: ADVANCED / AUTHENTIC READ · LISTEN · THINK · CREATE EDUCATION & VALUES CRITICAL THINKING
Value focus: Education is not preparation only for exams; it is preparation for life. Its purpose is not to fill a bucket, but to light a fire.
LEAD-IN:

You have spent over 10 years in education. If someone asked you «What is school FOR?» — what would you say?

Look at the four voices below. They come from:

THE TEXT · FIVE VOICES

VOICE 1. Sir Ken Robinson (British educationalist, 1950–2020)

«Many highly talented, brilliant, creative people think they're not — because the thing they were good at at school wasn't valued, or was actually stigmatised. Our education system has mined our minds in the way that we strip-mine the earth: for a particular commodity. And for the future, it won't serve us. We have to rethink the fundamental principles on which we're educating our children.

There is a wonderful story. A little girl was in a drawing lesson. She was six, and she was at the back, drawing. The teacher said, «What are you drawing?» And the girl said, «I'm drawing a picture of God.» And the teacher said, «But nobody knows what God looks like.» And the girl said, «They will in a minute.»

Children are not afraid to be wrong. But by the time they are adults, most children have lost that capacity. They have become frightened of being wrong. We run our schools like factories. We stigmatise mistakes. And the result is that we are educating people out of their creative capacities.»

VOICE 2. Malala Yousafzai (Pakistani activist, Nobel Prize laureate, 1997–)

«One child, one teacher, one book, one pen can change the world.»

«When I was in Swat, I saw that education was a weapon against fear. The Taliban were afraid of books. They were afraid of girls going to school. Why? Because education gives you the power to question. To question authority. To question tradition. To question your own fate.

Some people think that education is about getting a job. It is that, but it is also much more. Education is freedom. It is the right to choose your own path. It is the right to say: I am more than what others expect of me.

I speak not for myself, but for those without a voice… those who have fought for their right to learn, to live in equality, peace, and dignity.»

VOICE 3. Confucius (Chinese philosopher, 551–479 BC)

«I hear and I forget. I see and I remember. I do and I understand.»

«Education breeds confidence. Confidence breeds hope. Hope breeds peace.»

«The will to learn, not the score, is the true mark of a scholar. A man who has made a mistake and does not correct it is making another mistake.»

«Do not impose on others what you yourself do not desire. In this way, education becomes humanity.»

VOICE 4. A Folk Story (Traditional, origin unknown)

A young woman approached a master potter.

«I want to learn from you,» she said. «How long will it take?»

The master smiled. «If you study with me for one year, you will be a beginner. If you study with me for ten years, you will be skilled. If you study with me your whole life, you will become a vessel that others can drink from.»

The woman was confused. «But I just want to make pots. Good pots. Why must I study my whole life?»

The master picked up a lump of clay. «This clay is you,» he said. «The pot is your work. But the water inside the pot — that is your spirit. If you only learn technique, you will make empty pots. If you learn patience, humility, and love, your pots will hold water that quenches the thirst of others.»

He placed the clay in her hands.

«Education is not about becoming a potter. It is about becoming a vessel.»

VOICE 5. A Modern Voice (You)

After reading these four voices, return to your sentence from the Lead-In.

Would you change it now? Write your final definition:

«To me, the purpose of education is ________________________________________.»
